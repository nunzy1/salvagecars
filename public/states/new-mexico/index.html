New Mexico Salvage Vehicle Guidance

Understand New Mexico Salvage Titles, Salvage Certificates, nonrepairable vehicles, rebuilding and inspection requirements, owner-retained total losses, and what you should verify before buying a damaged vehicle.

What you should know

New Mexico has specific rules for vehicles declared salvage or nonrepairable.

Unlike some states, New Mexico does not use a single fixed percentage of a vehicle’s value to determine salvage status. Generally, a vehicle may be classified as salvage when the owner, lienholder, leasing company, or insurer determines that repairing it would not be economically worthwhile.

An insurer’s salvage determination can establish the vehicle’s status regardless of the relationship between repair costs and its value.

New Mexico distinguishes between a Salvage Title and a Salvage Certificate. A vehicle with a salvage-branded title may be eligible for road operation, but a vehicle with a Salvage Certificate cannot be operated on public roads.

To convert a Salvage Certificate into a Salvage Title, the New Mexico Motor Vehicle Division (MVD) requires a certified safety inspection and VIN verification. However, the MVD also states that New Mexico has no certified safety inspection station program. Its published guidance therefore does not establish a clear way for owners to obtain the required safety certification.

Before purchasing or rebuilding a vehicle with a Salvage Certificate, contact the MVD to determine whether a currently accepted inspection and title-conversion procedure is available. Do not assume that completing repairs will make the vehicle eligible for registration.

A vehicle legally designated nonrepairable cannot be rebuilt or restored for operation on New Mexico roads.

Understand New Mexico terminology

Salvage Vehicle

A salvage vehicle is generally a damaged vehicle that the owner, lienholder, leasing company, or insurer determines is uneconomical to repair and that meets New Mexico’s legal definition. Certain insurance total-loss determinations may establish salvage status even if the vehicle is later repaired.

Certain hail-damaged vehicles may be excluded under applicable MVD rules.

Salvage Title

A Salvage Title is a Certificate of Title branded SALVAGE. The MVD states that a vehicle with a Salvage Title may be operated on public roads if it satisfies applicable registration, insurance, equipment, and other legal requirements.

The salvage brand remains part of the vehicle’s title history.

Salvage Certificate

A Salvage Certificate is not the same as a Salvage Title. A vehicle with a Salvage Certificate cannot be operated on public roads.

The MVD says conversion requires a certified safety inspection and VIN verification, but it also states that New Mexico has no certified safety inspection station program. Its published guidance does not establish a clear way to obtain the required certification.

Contact the MVD before buying or rebuilding a vehicle with a Salvage Certificate to determine whether a currently accepted conversion procedure is available.

Nonrepairable Vehicle

A nonrepairable vehicle generally has no meaningful resale value except as parts or scrap metal, has been designated for dismantling or destruction, or meets other specific legal conditions. Certain substantially stripped or severely burned vehicles may qualify.

New Mexico issues a Nonrepairable Vehicle Certificate. A vehicle with this designation cannot be repaired, reconstructed, or restored for highway operation.

Rebuilt or Reconstructed Vehicle

New Mexico also uses REBUILT for certain reconstructed vehicles, including vehicles assembled or reconstructed using an existing chassis and other components.

These procedures are not necessarily the same as converting a Salvage Certificate into a road-eligible title. Do not assume that every vehicle described as rebuilt has completed the same legal process.

It depends on the vehicle’s legal classification and current ownership document.

Vehicle with a Salvage Title

A salvage-branded Certificate of Title may permit road operation when the vehicle meets applicable registration and equipment requirements.

Vehicle with a Salvage Certificate

A vehicle with a Salvage Certificate cannot be operated on public roads. The MVD identifies inspection requirements for converting the certificate into a title but does not establish a clear way to obtain the required safety certification.

Vehicle with a Nonrepairable Vehicle Certificate

A nonrepairable vehicle cannot be restored for highway operation in New Mexico.

Before purchasing, ask: What exact title or other ownership document does the vehicle have, and will the MVD issue the title and registration needed for ordinary road use?

How do I get a New Mexico salvage vehicle back on the road?

There is no single rebuilding process that applies to every vehicle described as salvage. First identify the current ownership document and confirm whether the vehicle can legally be titled and registered.

  1. Obtain the current title or other acceptable ownership document.
  2. Confirm whether the vehicle is classified as salvage or nonrepairable.
  3. Ask the MVD whether the ownership document permits registration or requires conversion.
  4. Verify whether the required inspections can actually be completed before investing in repairs.
  5. Complete necessary repairs and meet applicable safety and equipment requirements.
  6. Keep receipts and ownership records for replacement parts.
  7. Complete any required VIN verification and other available inspections.
  8. Submit the ownership document and any additional forms the MVD requires.
  9. Pay applicable title, tax, and registration fees.
  10. Obtain the appropriate title and registration before operating the vehicle on public roads.

Important limitation for Salvage Certificates

The MVD requires a certified safety inspection and VIN verification to convert a Salvage Certificate into a Salvage Title, but states that New Mexico has no certified safety inspection station program. Its published instructions therefore leave the practical inspection process unclear.

Do not assume that repairs alone will make the vehicle eligible for registration. Ask the MVD which requirements apply to the vehicle’s current title or other ownership document and whether a procedure is available to make the vehicle eligible for registration.

What inspections and verification are required?

VIN verification

New Mexico generally requires VIN verification for vehicles brought into the state from another jurisdiction. MVD field offices may perform VIN inspections, although availability varies by location. Certain reconstructed vehicles require a more detailed Level 3 VIN inspection.

Safety inspection for Salvage Certificate conversion

The MVD lists both a certified safety inspection and VIN verification as requirements for converting a Salvage Certificate to a Salvage Title. However, it also states that New Mexico does not have a certified safety inspection station program.

Before purchasing or rebuilding, ask the MVD whether an accepted inspection and title-conversion procedure is currently available.

Reconstructed vehicle inspections

Certain reconstructed vehicles require additional ownership documents and a Level 3 VIN inspection. Requirements depend on how the vehicle was constructed and its ownership history.

What the inspections do not establish

VIN verification helps establish vehicle identity and documentation. Government inspections should not be treated as a comprehensive guarantee of repair quality, mechanical condition, or long-term safety.

Does completing New Mexico’s title or inspection requirements mean the vehicle is a good purchase?

No. A title or inspection establishes only that particular legal or administrative requirements have been met. It does not guarantee that previous damage was repaired correctly.

Before buying, consider an independent evaluation of:

  • Structural repairs
  • Frame or unibody alignment
  • Airbags and restraint systems
  • Brakes, steering, and suspension
  • Electrical systems
  • Water or flood damage
  • Mechanical condition
  • Wheel alignment and tire wear
  • Rust, corrosion, and evidence of poor-quality body repairs
  • Replacement component quality

Ask for the original damage information, repair invoices, photographs, and available inspection records.

The important question is not simply:

Does the vehicle have a title?

It is also:

What happened to the vehicle, how was it repaired, and what condition is it in now?

What repair and parts documentation should I keep?

Keep documentation establishing ownership, identifying the vehicle and major components, and explaining the work performed. Useful records include:

  • The original title or salvage ownership document
  • Insurance damage assessments and settlement documents
  • Photographs before, during, and after repairs
  • Receipts for replacement parts and donor vehicle information
  • Repair invoices
  • Airbag and restraint-system documentation
  • VIN inspection records
  • Any required reconstructed-vehicle forms
  • MVD correspondence confirming title eligibility
  • The final title and registration documents

Not every item is mandatory for every vehicle. Reconstructed vehicles may require specific ownership affirmations, component invoices, equipment affirmations, and inspection documents.

For a vehicle with a Salvage Certificate, written confirmation of its eligibility for conversion may be especially important before repairs begin.

What if I keep my vehicle after the insurance company declares it a total loss?

New Mexico allows an owner to retain a vehicle after a total-loss insurance settlement, but the appropriate salvage or nonrepairable designation still applies.

If the owner keeps the vehicle and accepts a reduced insurance payment, MVD procedures require the ownership document to be marked SALVAGE or NONREPAIRABLE, as applicable. The required information must also be submitted to the MVD.

The Notice of Owner Retained Vehicle (MVD10651) documents the insurance settlement and the owner’s acknowledgment of applicable title requirements.

Before keeping the vehicle, determine:

  • Whether the insurer classified it as salvage or nonrepairable
  • Which ownership document the MVD will issue
  • Whether the existing title must be surrendered or branded
  • Whether a lienholder must participate
  • Whether the vehicle can legally be repaired and registered
  • Whether inspections or title conversion are required
  • Whether continued road operation is permitted

If the insurer takes title and the original owner later buys the salvage vehicle back, the MVD treats the transaction as a title change and requires salvage branding.

Keeping possession of a totaled vehicle does not eliminate its salvage or nonrepairable status.

Is there an age exemption from New Mexico’s salvage process?

New Mexico does not provide a general exemption from salvage classification simply because a vehicle is old. The state’s definition focuses on the damage, whether repair is economically worthwhile, and applicable insurance determinations.

Certain hail-damage exclusions may apply under MVD rules, but age alone does not establish an exemption.

Before buying an older damaged vehicle, verify:

  • Whether an insurer declared it a total loss
  • Whether it has a salvage or nonrepairable designation
  • Whether the ownership document permits registration
  • Whether the MVD requires additional documentation or inspections
  • Whether it is eligible for road use

Do not assume that an older vehicle is exempt from New Mexico’s salvage requirements solely because of its age.

What about flood-damaged vehicles?

Flood damage can make a vehicle uneconomical to repair and may result in a salvage or nonrepairable designation. New Mexico’s title-processing guidance also recognizes flood-related title brands from other states.

Significant water damage can cause problems that remain hidden after cleaning and cosmetic repairs. Before buying, investigate:

  • Electrical wiring and connectors
  • Electronic control modules
  • Airbags and restraint systems
  • Rust and corrosion
  • Interior materials and carpeting
  • Mechanical components
  • Mold or contamination
  • Evidence of previous water intrusion

Ask whether the vehicle has ever been declared a total loss because of water damage. Check title history and available insurance records. A vehicle-history report may help, but it may not identify every instance of water damage.

A clean-looking interior does not establish that a flood-damaged vehicle is safe or reliable.

What if the vehicle comes from another state?

New Mexico generally requires VIN verification for vehicles brought into the state. The MVD recognizes salvage-equivalent brands from other jurisdictions, including certain flood- and hail-damage designations.

However, the exact ownership document matters. The MVD states that an out-of-state Salvage Certificate that is not a Certificate of Title cannot be accepted as a Certificate of Title.

Before buying an out-of-state vehicle, determine:

  • Which state issued the ownership document
  • Whether it is a Certificate of Title, Salvage Certificate, or another ownership record
  • Whether it has salvage, rebuilt, flood, hail, junk, or nonrepairable designations
  • Whether the MVD accepts the document for the intended transaction
  • Whether the vehicle can be registered for road use
  • Whether VIN verification or other inspections are required
  • Whether there is a lienholder
  • Whether the seller can provide a complete chain of ownership

Contact the MVD before buying a vehicle with an unfamiliar or restricted ownership document.

Transferring a vehicle between states does not remove its salvage history.

What should I check before buying a New Mexico salvage vehicle?

  • What exact ownership document does the vehicle have?
  • Is it a Salvage Title, Salvage Certificate, or Nonrepairable Vehicle Certificate?
  • Does the MVD accept the document?
  • Can the vehicle legally be registered and operated on New Mexico roads?
  • If it has a Salvage Certificate, is an accepted conversion procedure currently available?
  • Why was it declared salvage, and was there an insurance total-loss settlement?
  • Has the vehicle been repaired, and who performed the work?
  • Are repair invoices and parts receipts available?
  • Has it undergone required VIN verification or other inspections?
  • Does it have out-of-state title brands or evidence of flood damage?
  • Has an independent mechanic or body specialist evaluated it?
  • Are there liens or ownership issues?
  • Can you obtain the insurance coverage and financing you need?
  • What repair, transportation, inspection, tax, title, registration, and other costs remain?

For an unrepaired vehicle, ask:

Can this vehicle legally be repaired, titled, registered, and operated on New Mexico roads?

For a repaired vehicle, ask:

Can I verify its ownership document, title history, repair quality, and eligibility for road use before buying it?

What should I know if I later sell or transfer the vehicle?

New Mexico requires appropriate salvage or nonrepairable branding before a qualifying vehicle is transferred. A seller generally cannot transfer a salvage or nonrepairable vehicle using an ordinary unbranded ownership document when the law requires a special designation.

Once a New Mexico title has been branded Salvage, subsequent title transfers must retain the brand.

A nonrepairable vehicle may be transferred only to certain licensed businesses that dismantle, recycle, or otherwise dispose of vehicles.

Before selling:

  • Confirm that the ownership document accurately identifies the vehicle’s status
  • Do not represent a salvage vehicle as having an ordinary unbranded title
  • Verify that the buyer can legally receive the vehicle in its current classification
  • Properly assign the ownership document and resolve liens
  • Provide available damage and repair records
  • Explain unresolved inspection or registration requirements

A vehicle designated nonrepairable cannot be sold as eligible for restoration and ordinary highway use in New Mexico.

About this guidance

SalvageCars.com provides general educational information to help you understand salvage vehicles, identify questions you may need to answer, and find the government agencies and official resources responsible for the applicable requirements.

This information is not legal advice and is not a substitute for guidance from the New Mexico Motor Vehicle Division or another government agency. Requirements, forms, fees, and procedures can change, and the rules that apply can depend on the particular vehicle and transaction.

Before buying, rebuilding, selling, titling, or registering a salvage vehicle, verify the current requirements with the appropriate New Mexico agency.
