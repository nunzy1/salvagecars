North Carolina Salvage Vehicle Guidance

Understand North Carolina salvage and rebuilt vehicle rules, preliminary and final inspections, title brands, and whether a damaged vehicle can legally return to the road.

What you should know

North Carolina generally defines a salvage motor vehicle as one damaged by collision or another occurrence when the cost of repairs, including parts and labor, needed to make it safe for public-road use would exceed 75% of its fair market retail value.

A qualifying salvage vehicle can potentially be rebuilt and titled for road use. A junk vehicle, however, cannot be titled or registered.

Vehicle age matters. For vehicles up to and including six model years old, North Carolina generally requires a preliminary inspection before rebuilding and a final inspection after the work is completed.

These inspections are anti-theft measures. North Carolina law expressly says they do not certify the safety or roadworthiness of the vehicle.

If an insurance company licensed in North Carolina declared the vehicle a total loss, the title and registration can also carry a TOTAL LOSS CLAIM notation, with a permanent marker required on applicable rebuilt vehicles.

Understand North Carolina terminology

Salvage motor vehicle

North Carolina defines a salvage motor vehicle as one damaged by collision or another occurrence when the cost of repairs necessary to make it safe for public-road use would exceed 75% of its fair market retail value. The calculation includes parts and labor.

For vehicles more than six model years old, North Carolina law excludes the cost of replacing the airbag restraint system from the repair-cost calculation used for salvage branding.

Salvage rebuilt vehicle

North Carolina defines a Salvage Rebuilt Vehicle as a salvage vehicle that has been rebuilt for title and registration. When applicable, the title and registration records identify that history.

Reconstructed vehicle

A reconstructed vehicle is not simply another name for a salvage rebuilt vehicle. North Carolina defines a reconstructed vehicle as a vehicle that has been materially altered from its original construction through removal, addition, or substitution of essential parts.

The classification can also become important when the required salvage inspection sequence was not followed.

Flood vehicle

North Carolina defines a flood vehicle as a vehicle that has been submerged or partially submerged in water to the extent that damage occurred to the body, engine, transmission, or differential.

Junk vehicle

A junk vehicle is incapable of highway operation or use and has no resale value except as a source of parts or scrap. North Carolina does not title or register a junk vehicle. A vehicle branded junk in another state also cannot be titled or registered in North Carolina.

Can this vehicle return to the road?

Possibly. A qualifying salvage vehicle can potentially be rebuilt and retitled.

Before buying, answer three questions:

Is the vehicle salvage rather than junk or otherwise unregisterable?

How old is the vehicle for purposes of North Carolina's inspection rules?

If the vehicle requires a preliminary inspection, did that inspection occur before repairs began?

That third question is especially important when buying a vehicle that someone else has already started rebuilding.

How does North Carolina count the six-model-year period?

North Carolina counts the vehicle's model year as the first model year and the current calendar year as the final model year.

Because the applicable group changes as the calendar year changes, verify whether the particular vehicle falls within the six-model-year inspection window before rebuilding it rather than relying only on an informal statement of the vehicle's age.

How do I get a salvage vehicle back on the road?

  1. Confirm the vehicle's ownership document and title status.
  2. Determine whether the vehicle falls within North Carolina's six-model-year inspection requirement.
  3. If required, arrange the preliminary inspection before rebuilding the vehicle.
  4. Rebuild the vehicle.
  5. Keep documentation identifying the parts and major components used or replaced.
  6. Document the labor and total repair cost.
  7. Complete the required rebuilder or owner affidavit.
  8. Arrange the required final inspection.
  9. Provide the vehicle and required ownership, repair, parts, and inspection documentation.
  10. Complete the applicable North Carolina title application process.
  11. Complete any separate safety or emissions inspection requirements that apply to registration.
  12. If approved, receive the applicable North Carolina title and registration reflecting the vehicle's required branding.

Vehicles more than six model years old can follow a different inspection path, so do not assume that every rebuilt salvage vehicle requires exactly the same inspections.

What happens at the preliminary inspection?

For vehicles subject to North Carolina's newer-vehicle inspection requirement, the preliminary inspection occurs before rebuilding.

The inspection is performed through the North Carolina State Highway Patrol's Investigative Services Unit. It establishes a record of the vehicle before reconstruction and supports the state's anti-theft and vehicle-identification process.

If you are considering buying a partially rebuilt North Carolina salvage vehicle that falls within the applicable age range, determine whether this preliminary inspection occurred before the work began.

What happens at the final inspection?

After the rebuild is completed, a vehicle subject to the requirement must undergo the final inspection. This inspection is also part of North Carolina's anti-theft process.

The state reviews the rebuilt vehicle and its documentation, including information about repaired or replaced parts and major components.

For a vehicle declared a total loss by an insurance company licensed in North Carolina, the applicable final-inspection process also includes placement of the permanent TOTAL LOSS CLAIM VEHICLE marker in the doorjamb.

Do these inspections certify that the vehicle is safe?

No.

North Carolina law expressly states that the preliminary and final inspections required for these newer damaged vehicles serve as anti-theft measures and do not certify the safety or roadworthiness of the vehicle.

A vehicle that has completed North Carolina's salvage inspection process should still be independently evaluated for structural repair quality, mechanical condition, steering and suspension, wheel alignment, electrical systems, airbags and restraint systems, corrosion or water damage, replacement components, insurance availability, and future resale implications.

Title eligibility and vehicle condition are different questions.

Why are parts and repair records important?

North Carolina's rebuilt-title process requires detailed information about the rebuild.

The supporting affidavit can require disclosure of parts used or replaced, major components replaced, hours of labor, hourly labor rate, total repair cost, and applicable TOTAL LOSS CLAIM VEHICLE marker information.

The state maintains these affidavits and makes them available to people researching the salvage and repair history of the vehicle. For newer vehicles undergoing preliminary and final inspection, documentation of parts used in the rebuild also supports the state's anti-theft review.

If you buy a partially rebuilt vehicle, determine whether the required documentation exists before taking over the project.

What documents might I need?

Vehicle ownership document

The title, salvage certificate, or other document establishing ownership and the vehicle's current title status.

Preliminary inspection report

Required for applicable vehicles up to and including six model years old.

Rebuilder or owner affidavit

Documents applicable parts, major components, labor, and repair costs.

Parts receipts and ownership documentation

Supports the source and ownership of parts and major components used during rebuilding.

Final inspection report

Required for applicable vehicles in the six-model-year inspection group.

North Carolina title application documents

Used to apply for the resulting certificate of title.

Safety or emissions inspection documentation, when applicable

Separate registration-related inspection requirements can apply after the salvage-title process.

What happens if the preliminary inspection was skipped?

This is one of the most important North Carolina-specific issues for a salvage buyer.

North Carolina's administrative rules provide that when a qualifying North Carolina salvage vehicle six model years old or newer is being transferred for rebuilding, the title application should include the required preliminary inspection, rebuilder or owner affidavit, and final inspection documentation.

If the required inspection documentation is not submitted, an additional vehicle examination can be required and the resulting vehicle can be branded Reconstructed rather than Salvage Rebuilt.

Do not assume you can rebuild the vehicle first and sort out the inspections afterward.

What does TOTAL LOSS CLAIM mean?

North Carolina separately identifies vehicles declared total losses by insurance companies licensed and approved to do business in the state.

The title and registration card carry the notation TOTAL LOSS CLAIM.

For an applicable vehicle that is subsequently reconstructed, repaired, or rebuilt, North Carolina also requires a permanent tamperproof marker in the vehicle's doorjamb stating TOTAL LOSS CLAIM VEHICLE. The Division places the marker as part of the applicable final-inspection process.

The notation is separate from other title brands such as Salvage Rebuilt, Reconstructed, or Flood. A vehicle can therefore carry more than one piece of title-history information.

Can a repaired vehicle ever receive an unbranded title?

North Carolina law contains circumstances in which a damaged vehicle can receive an unbranded title if its repair cost does not exceed the statutory 75% threshold and the required disclosures and, when applicable, inspections are completed.

For vehicles up to and including six model years old, the vehicle must complete the required preliminary and final inspections. For vehicles more than six model years old, the statute allows retitling without those inspections when the required affidavit is provided.

This is one reason not to assume that every vehicle involved in a collision or total-loss claim will ultimately carry exactly the same title brand. A TOTAL LOSS CLAIM notation can also apply independently when the vehicle was declared a total loss by a qualifying insurer.

What if the vehicle came from another state?

North Carolina generally carries forward an out-of-state vehicle brand using the nearest applicable North Carolina brand. A vehicle branded junk in another state cannot be titled or registered in North Carolina.

Out-of-state branded salvage vehicles seeking North Carolina titles can also be subject to examination.

  • Inspect the actual ownership document.
  • Identify every existing brand.
  • Determine whether the vehicle is salvage, rebuilt, reconstructed, flood, junk, or another classification.
  • Confirm that North Carolina will accept the ownership document.
  • Determine whether an additional vehicle examination is required.
  • Verify what North Carolina brand will apply.

Do not rely solely on the auction listing's description.

What about flood vehicles?

North Carolina has a specific Flood Vehicle title classification. The definition applies when a vehicle has been submerged or partially submerged to the extent that damage occurred to its body, engine, transmission, or differential.

Flood branding identifies title history. It does not establish that all water-related problems were discovered or repaired.

  • corrosion in wiring and connectors
  • electronic module damage
  • water contamination
  • mold and interior damage
  • airbag and restraint-system components
  • engine and drivetrain exposure
  • long-term electrical reliability

What should I check before buying a salvage vehicle in North Carolina?

  • What is the exact ownership document?
  • What brands or notations appear on it?
  • Is the vehicle salvage, junk, reconstructed, flood, or subject to another classification?
  • Was it declared a total loss?
  • Does the TOTAL LOSS CLAIM notation apply?
  • How many model years old is the vehicle under North Carolina's statutory counting method?
  • Does it require preliminary and final inspections?
  • If a preliminary inspection was required, did it occur before repairs began?
  • Has someone already partially or completely rebuilt the vehicle?
  • What parts and major components were replaced?
  • Are receipts and repair records available?
  • Are the VIN and component identification numbers intact?
  • What was the documented repair cost?
  • Does the vehicle need an additional vehicle examination?
  • Can you obtain the insurance coverage you want?
  • What title, registration, inspection, repair, tax, and transportation costs remain?
  • Has the quality and safety of the completed repairs been independently evaluated?

For North Carolina, one of the most important questions is:

Did this vehicle follow the correct inspection sequence before and after rebuilding?

What should I know if I later sell or transfer the vehicle?

North Carolina title branding is intended to disclose significant vehicle history. Once the Division has issued a branded title for a vehicle, subsequent North Carolina titles continue to reflect that branding.

TOTAL LOSS CLAIM information also remains relevant when applicable.

If you later sell or transfer a rebuilt vehicle, accurately represent its title and damage history and verify the disclosure and transfer requirements that apply to the particular transaction. Do not describe a rebuilt vehicle in a way that contradicts the branding or other history shown in its official records.

About this guidance

SalvageCars.com provides general educational information to help you understand salvage vehicles, identify questions you may need to answer, and find the government agencies and official resources responsible for the applicable requirements.

This information is not legal advice and is not a substitute for guidance from the North Carolina Division of Motor Vehicles, North Carolina State Highway Patrol, or another appropriate government agency. Requirements, forms, fees, inspection procedures, title classifications, and other requirements can change and can depend on the particular vehicle and transaction.

Before buying, rebuilding, selling, titling, or registering a salvage vehicle, verify the current requirements with the appropriate government agency.
