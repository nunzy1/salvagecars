Tennessee Salvage Vehicle Guidance

Understand Tennessee salvage and non-repairable certificates, the rebuilt-vehicle process, documentation requirements, anti-theft inspection, and what must happen before a salvage vehicle can return to road use.

What you should know

Tennessee distinguishes between a Salvage Vehicle that may potentially be rebuilt for road use and a Non-repairable Vehicle that cannot.

For passenger vehicles covered by Tennessee's salvage law, a vehicle is generally considered salvage when it has been wrecked, destroyed, or damaged to the extent that repair costs exceed 75% of its retail value.

A vehicle with a Salvage Certificate cannot be registered or driven on Tennessee roads until it successfully completes the rebuilt-vehicle process. A Non-repairable Vehicle cannot be titled or registered for road use.

If a salvage vehicle qualifies for a rebuilt title, the resulting Tennessee title is branded Rebuilt Vehicle — Anti-Theft Inspected. The salvage history does not disappear.

Documentation matters throughout this process. Tennessee requires photographs of the vehicle in its damaged condition and detailed receipts for applicable replacement parts.

Understand Tennessee terminology

Salvage Vehicle

Tennessee defines a salvage vehicle as a passenger motor vehicle that has been wrecked, destroyed, or damaged to the extent that repair costs exceed 75% of the vehicle's retail value.

A Salvage Certificate establishes ownership but does not authorize the vehicle to be registered or driven on Tennessee roads. A salvage vehicle may potentially be repaired and converted to a rebuilt vehicle.

Non-repairable Vehicle

A Non-repairable Vehicle is a passenger motor vehicle that is incapable of safe operation on roads or highways and has no resale value except as a source of parts or scrap, or that has been irreversibly designated for parts or scrap.

A Non-repairable Certificate establishes ownership but does not provide a path back to ordinary road use.

Rebuilt Vehicle

A salvage vehicle repaired in accordance with applicable requirements can enter Tennessee's rebuilt-vehicle process. After approval, the vehicle receives a title branded Rebuilt Vehicle — Anti-Theft Inspected. The rebuilt designation remains part of its title history.

Can this vehicle return to the road?

Potentially, if it has a Salvage Certificate or another ownership document Tennessee accepts for the rebuilt process. A Non-repairable Vehicle cannot.

Before buying a damaged or salvage vehicle, first ask:

What exact ownership document does this vehicle have?

Then ask:

Does Tennessee allow a vehicle with that document to enter the rebuilt process?

Do not rely only on descriptions such as “repairable,” “salvage,” or “rebuildable.” A vehicle can be physically repairable while its ownership document prevents it from legally returning to road use.

How do I get a Tennessee salvage vehicle back on the road?

For a vehicle eligible for the rebuilt process, the general path is:

  1. Establish ownership with the appropriate salvage ownership document.
  2. Photograph the vehicle in its damaged condition before rebuilding.
  3. Repair the vehicle in accordance with applicable requirements.
  4. Keep detailed receipts and ownership documentation for replacement components.
  5. Complete the Application for Motor Vehicle Identification Certification for Rebuilt Vehicles.
  6. Submit the salvage ownership document, application, photographs, parts documentation, and required fee to the Tennessee Department of Revenue.
  7. Complete an Anti-Theft Unit inspection if the Department requires one.
  8. After approval, follow the Department's instructions for issuance of the Tennessee rebuilt title.

For an individual applicant, the Department says that after approval it provides documentation authorizing the applicant to obtain the rebuilt title through the county clerk. If a physical inspection is required, the Special Investigation Unit contacts the applicant to arrange it.

Does every rebuilt vehicle receive a physical inspection?

Current Tennessee Department of Revenue guidance indicates that a physical inspection may be required rather than stating that every approved application automatically receives one.

If a physical inspection is required, an agent with the Department's Special Investigation Unit contacts the applicant to arrange it. Current guidance also describes a path for an individual whose application is approved without a physical inspection: the applicant takes the approval letter to the county clerk to apply for a rebuilt title.

Because the Department determines what is required for a particular application, do not assume another applicant's experience will determine what happens with your vehicle.

What is the rebuilt inspection for?

Tennessee describes the rebuilt process as addressing anti-theft and fraud concerns. The application requires certification concerning the rebuild and requires documentation establishing where applicable replacement components came from.

A rebuilt title should not be treated as a substitute for an independent mechanical or structural evaluation.

Why should I photograph the vehicle before repairs?

Tennessee requires color photographs showing the vehicle in its damaged condition. Current Department instructions call for photographs showing all four quadrants of the wrecked vehicle as part of the rebuilt application.

If you buy a salvage vehicle after someone else has already started repairing it, ask whether the required damaged-condition photographs exist before completing the purchase.

Tennessee provides a procedure for situations in which the required damaged-condition photographs are unavailable, but that procedure should not be treated as a reason to skip the photographs when rebuilding a vehicle from the beginning.

Why are parts receipts important?

Tennessee requires receipts for replacement parts identified in the rebuilt application.

For parts removed from another vehicle, current Department instructions require the receipt to include the buyer's and seller's complete names and mailing addresses and the year, make, and VIN of the vehicle from which the parts were removed.

For parts taken from an applicant's own in-house supply, the Department requires proof of ownership of the donor vehicle. If a replacement part is new, the receipt must indicate that it was new.

If you are considering buying a partially rebuilt vehicle, inspect the parts documentation before buying it.

What documents might I need?

Salvage ownership documentFor a Tennessee vehicle, this will ordinarily be the Salvage Certificate. An acceptable out-of-state salvage title can also be used in Tennessee's rebuilt process.
Application for Motor Vehicle Identification Certification for Rebuilt VehiclesThe principal Department of Revenue application for converting an eligible salvage vehicle to rebuilt status. The vehicle must be complete before submission.
Damaged-condition photographsCurrent instructions require color photographs showing all four quadrants of the vehicle in its wrecked condition.
Parts receipts and donor-vehicle documentationKeep required documentation for applicable components installed during the rebuild.

Vehicle history and individual circumstances can affect what the Department requires. Use the current Department application and instructions when preparing a rebuilt submission.

Can I drive a salvage vehicle while I rebuild it?

No. Tennessee Department of Revenue guidance states that a salvage vehicle cannot be driven on Tennessee roads until the rebuilt process has been completed.

The state also says there is no required time limit for completing the rebuilt process, but the vehicle cannot be driven during that period. Plan accordingly for transportation if the vehicle needs to be moved before rebuilt approval.

What if I retained my own vehicle after the insurance company totaled it?

Keeping the vehicle does not allow the owner to avoid Tennessee's salvage requirements.

When an insurer does not take ownership of a damaged passenger vehicle that must be titled as salvage or non-repairable, Tennessee requires the insurer to notify the owner of the obligation to apply for the appropriate certificate and to notify the Department.

The insurer may instead withhold payment of the claim until the owner applies for the required salvage or non-repairable certificate.

Tennessee does not provide a general exception allowing a qualifying totaled vehicle to remain normally registered and driven merely because the original owner kept it.

What if the vehicle came from another state?

Tennessee recognizes out-of-state salvage and rebuilt documents, but the exact document matters.

Out-of-state salvage title

Current Department guidance says an out-of-state salvage title can be submitted with the required rebuilt documents for Tennessee's pre-certification process. You do not first have to exchange an acceptable out-of-state salvage title for a Tennessee Salvage Certificate before beginning the rebuilt process.

Different terminology from another state

Not every state uses the same terminology. When an out-of-state title carries a brand that does not correspond clearly to Tennessee's Salvage, Rebuilt, or Non-repairable categories, Tennessee Vehicle Services researches the previous state's designation to determine the appropriate Tennessee brand. That review can include contacting the previous state for an explanation or requiring a corrected title.

Nonrepairable or similarly restrictive documents

Treat these as a major warning. Tennessee's own Non-repairable Certificate does not provide a path back to road use.

If another state's ownership document says Nonrepairable, Parts Only, Junk, Certificate of Destruction, or uses another restrictive designation, do not assume Tennessee will treat it as an ordinary salvage title. Have Tennessee Vehicle Services determine how the document will be treated before buying the vehicle for a rebuilding project.

What about the 10-year rule?

Tennessee's public guidance says that when a vehicle is deemed a qualifying total loss and is less than 10 years old, the owner can apply for a Salvage or Non-repairable Certificate.

Age can therefore affect the initial salvage-certificate requirements. However, age should not be used as a shortcut when evaluating a vehicle that already has salvage history.

Before buying an older vehicle with salvage history, determine what ownership document or salvage record exists and what Tennessee requires for that specific vehicle.

Does rebuilt approval mean the vehicle is a good purchase?

No. Tennessee's rebuilt process addresses title status, identity, documentation, anti-theft concerns, and required certifications. It does not eliminate the need for a buyer to evaluate the actual quality of the reconstruction.

Before buying a rebuilt vehicle, independently investigate structural repairs, steering and suspension, alignment, brakes, airbags and restraint systems, electrical systems, mechanical condition, flood damage, corrosion, replacement components, and evidence of incomplete or poorly performed repairs.

Also investigate whether you can obtain the insurance coverage you want and how the rebuilt title history could affect future resale.

What should I check before buying a Tennessee salvage vehicle?

  • What exact ownership document does the vehicle have?
  • Is it Salvage, Non-repairable, Rebuilt, or something else?
  • What caused the vehicle to be declared a total loss?
  • Is it legally eligible for Tennessee's rebuilt process?
  • Are photographs of the vehicle in its damaged condition available?
  • Has repair work already started?
  • Are receipts available for applicable replacement components?
  • Can donor-vehicle VIN information be documented where required?
  • Has the rebuilt application already been submitted?
  • Has Tennessee required or completed a physical inspection?
  • If it came from another state, how will Tennessee treat its existing title brand?
  • Has repair quality been independently evaluated?
  • Can you obtain the insurance coverage you want?
  • What repair, title, inspection, transportation, tax, registration, and other costs remain?

The central Tennessee question is:

Does this vehicle's ownership document permit it to enter Tennessee's rebuilt process, and is there enough documentation to establish what was repaired and where the replacement components came from?

What should I know if I later sell or transfer the vehicle?

A Tennessee rebuilt vehicle does not return to a clear title. The resulting title carries the rebuilt designation, and that history remains relevant when the vehicle is later transferred.

Tennessee also requires disclosure before the sale of a vehicle with a rebuilt title, salvage title, or salvage history in transactions covered by its motor-vehicle dealer rules, along with the purchaser's signed acknowledgment on the approved disclosure form.

If you later sell a rebuilt vehicle, accurately represent its title and salvage history and comply with the transfer and disclosure requirements that apply to your transaction.

About this guidance

SalvageCars.com provides general educational information to help you understand salvage vehicles, identify questions you may need to answer, and find the government agencies and official resources responsible for the applicable requirements.

This information is not legal advice and is not a substitute for guidance from the Tennessee Department of Revenue, Tennessee Vehicle Services, the Tennessee Motor Vehicle Commission, or another government agency. Requirements, forms, fees, inspection procedures, and title classifications can change, and the rules that apply can depend on the particular vehicle and transaction.

Before buying, rebuilding, selling, titling, or registering a salvage vehicle, verify the current requirements with the appropriate Tennessee agency.
