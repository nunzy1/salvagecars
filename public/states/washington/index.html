Washington Salvage Vehicle Guidance

Understand Washington salvage vehicles, WSP inspections, rebuilt-title requirements, major-component documentation, and whether a totaled vehicle can legally return to the road.

What you should know

Washington treats a vehicle as a salvage vehicle when it has been destroyed or declared a total loss and meets the state's salvage-vehicle definition.

Once a vehicle has been reported destroyed or declared a total loss, it cannot legally be driven or parked on public highways or roads until a new title has been issued.

A salvage vehicle can potentially return to the road after it has been repaired and the applicable Washington title and inspection requirements have been completed.

However, not every vehicle follows exactly the same process. Whether a Washington State Patrol inspection is required can depend on the vehicle's history, who retained or acquired it after the loss, and whether it came from another state.

Not every reissued title will necessarily carry the WA REBUILT brand either. Washington's branding rules depend partly on the vehicle's age and pre-loss market value.

Understand Washington terminology

Salvage vehicle

Washington defines a salvage vehicle as a vehicle whose title was surrendered because it was destroyed or declared a total loss, or for which documentation shows that it was declared salvage or damaged to the point that repairing it was considered uneconomical.

Washington's statutory definition also contains age and market-value qualifications.

Vehicles five years old or newer generally fall within the salvage definition when the other requirements are met. For vehicles 6 through 20 years old, Washington uses a market-value threshold as part of the definition.

The current Washington administrative rule lists that threshold as $12,220. Because Washington adjusts this amount over time, verify the current threshold rather than relying on an older dollar amount.

WA REBUILT

A vehicle reported destroyed and later issued a new Washington title may receive the brand WA REBUILT when it meets Washington's applicable salvage-branding criteria.

Not every previously damaged vehicle receives this brand. The applicable age, market-value, and title-history rules matter.

Destroyed vehicle

Washington uses the concept of a vehicle being reported destroyed as part of its total-loss process.

The registered or legal owner generally must report the destruction and surrender the title within 15 days. An insurance company generally must report a total loss within 15 days after settling the claim.

How do I get a Washington salvage vehicle back on the road?

The exact process depends on the vehicle's history, but a typical vehicle requiring a rebuilt-salvage inspection follows this path:

  1. Determine whether the vehicle is eligible to receive a new Washington title.
  2. Visit a Washington vehicle licensing office to determine the title requirements and whether a Washington State Patrol inspection is required.
  3. Obtain the Washington State Patrol Inspection Request Form from the licensing agent if an inspection is required.
  4. Repair or replace all damaged major component parts before scheduling the inspection.
  5. Keep the original ownership and parts documentation required for the vehicle and repairs.
  6. Make sure required equipment, including applicable airbags, has been restored.
  7. Schedule the required WSP VIN inspection.
  8. Bring the completed vehicle and all required original documentation to the inspection.
  9. If the vehicle passes, use the WSP inspection documentation and other required ownership documents to apply for a new Washington title.

The first practical step should usually be the vehicle licensing office. WSP tells rebuilt-vehicle applicants to verify that an inspection is required and obtain the Inspection Request Form from a DOL licensing agent before scheduling.

Does every rebuilt salvage vehicle require a Washington State Patrol inspection?

No. This is one of the more important Washington distinctions.

Washington law requires an inspection in several situations, including certain vehicles declared a total loss or salvage, vehicles rebuilt after the title was returned to DOL when the vehicle was not kept by the registered owner at the time of the loss, and certain vehicles presented with out-of-state salvage or total-loss documents.

The law also contains exceptions. An owner who retains a vehicle after a total-loss event may not necessarily follow the identical inspection path as someone who buys the totaled vehicle afterward.

Do not assume either that an inspection is required or that it is unnecessary based only on the word “salvage.” Take the ownership documents to a Washington vehicle licensing office and have the licensing agent determine which process applies.

What does the Washington State Patrol inspect?

For a rebuilt salvage vehicle that requires inspection, WSP verifies the vehicle's identity and examines the major component parts used in its reconstruction.

The inspection helps establish that:

  • the vehicle identification number agrees with the applicable ownership records
  • required major component parts have been repaired or replaced
  • major components used in the reconstruction were legally acquired
  • the applicant has the required ownership and parts documentation

All damaged major component parts must be repaired or replaced before inspection. WSP will not inspect an incomplete rebuilt salvage vehicle.

Major components include items such as the engine, transmission or transfer case, doors, front or rear clip, truck bed or box, hood, fenders, frame, cab, differentials, quarter panels, seats, bumpers, airbags, and catalytic converter.

If the vehicle was originally manufactured with airbags, WSP currently instructs rebuilders that the airbags must be replaced before inspection.

What marking does WSP put on a rebuilt vehicle?

For an applicable vehicle that completes the inspection process, a WSP VIN specialist securely attaches a permanent marking at the driver's door-latch pillar indicating that the vehicle was previously destroyed or declared a total loss.

Washington law makes removing that marking a Class C felony.

This marking is separate from the title brand and remains a physical indication of the vehicle's history.

Why are receipts and parts documentation especially important?

WSP verifies that major component parts used to reconstruct the vehicle were legally acquired.

Applicants should preserve the original documentation associated with both the vehicle and replacement components.

The current WSP guidance specifically warns that Internet receipts for used parts are not accepted.

For major components purchased online, WSP currently accepts Internet or electronic purchases when the component is a new aftermarket part purchased from a licensed business and the printed receipt contains the required seller, purchase, part, price, and tax information.

Internet auction or bid receipts for used components are not accepted.

If you are buying a partially rebuilt vehicle, ask for the parts documentation before completing the purchase. Missing documentation for major components can prevent the vehicle from successfully completing the inspection.

Can I drive a salvage vehicle to the inspection?

A vehicle declared salvage cannot legally be driven or parked on Washington public highways or roads until a new title has been issued.

If the vehicle needs to be moved to a WSP inspection, ask the vehicle licensing office what temporary operating authority, if any, applies. Depending on the circumstances, the vehicle may need to be transported rather than driven.

Do not assume that having a WSP inspection appointment by itself authorizes the vehicle to be driven on public roads.

What if the owner kept the vehicle after the insurance total loss?

Owner-retained total-loss vehicles deserve particular attention because Washington's inspection rules distinguish between some owner-retained vehicles and vehicles acquired by someone else after the loss.

If the registered owner keeps the vehicle after it is declared a total loss, the owner should take the applicable registration, Notice of Cancellation, and other required documentation to a vehicle licensing office to determine the title process.

Do not assume that the process for an owner-retained vehicle is identical to the process for a vehicle purchased after the total loss.

Ask who owned the vehicle at the time it was declared a total loss and what happened to the ownership record afterward.

What if the vehicle came from another state?

Washington carries forward many title brands from other states and jurisdictions.

A vehicle presented with out-of-state documents showing that it was a total loss or salvage vehicle may require a Washington inspection if a valid registration was not reissued by the other state after the salvage or total-loss declaration.

Before buying an out-of-state vehicle:

  • inspect the actual title or ownership document
  • identify every title brand
  • determine whether the other state reissued a registration after the salvage declaration
  • ask a Washington licensing agent whether WSP inspection is required
  • determine whether the ownership document is sufficient for a Washington title
  • verify that the major-component documentation will satisfy Washington requirements

The exact document matters more than the seller's description of the vehicle as “salvage,” “rebuilt,” or “repairable.”

Will the new title always say WA REBUILT?

No.

Washington's rebuilt branding rules depend on whether the vehicle meets the state's salvage-vehicle criteria.

Vehicles five years old or newer generally meet the age portion of the salvage definition. For vehicles 6 through 20 years old, the applicable market-value threshold can matter.

Washington's current administrative rule lists the market-value threshold as $12,220.

Because this amount changes periodically, verify the current threshold when evaluating a particular vehicle.

Washington also carries forward many brands from other jurisdictions, so an out-of-state vehicle can have title-history information that does not fit neatly into the Washington-only WA REBUILT description.

Does passing the WSP inspection mean the vehicle is a good purchase?

No.

The WSP inspection is important, but its purposes include vehicle identification, verifying legal ownership of major components, and confirming compliance with applicable inspection requirements.

Passing the inspection should not be treated as a warranty that the vehicle is a sound purchase or will be reliable over time.

Before buying a rebuilt vehicle, independently investigate structural repairs, steering and suspension, alignment, airbags and restraint systems, electrical systems, mechanical condition, corrosion or flood damage, replacement components, and incomplete repairs.

Also investigate whether you can obtain the insurance coverage you want and how the vehicle's title history could affect future resale.

What should I check before buying a salvage vehicle in Washington?

  • What is the exact ownership document?
  • Was the vehicle formally reported destroyed or declared a total loss?
  • Who owned the vehicle when the loss occurred?
  • Did the original registered owner retain it?
  • Does Washington require a WSP inspection?
  • Has the WSP Inspection Request Form been obtained?
  • Have all damaged major component parts been repaired or replaced?
  • If originally equipped with airbags, have they been replaced?
  • Are original receipts and invoices for major components available?
  • Were used major components purchased through an Internet source that may not satisfy WSP documentation requirements?
  • Has the vehicle already undergone WSP inspection?
  • Will the resulting title carry the WA REBUILT brand?
  • If it came from another state, what brands appear on the actual title?
  • Can you obtain the insurance coverage you want?
  • What costs remain?
  • Has the quality of the repairs been independently evaluated?

The central Washington question is:

What happened to this vehicle after it was declared a total loss, and what title and inspection path does that history require?

What should I know if I later sell or transfer the vehicle?

A vehicle's rebuilt or branded history remains relevant after it returns to the road.

If the Washington title carries the WA REBUILT brand, that brand remains part of the vehicle's title history. Washington also carries forward applicable brands from other jurisdictions.

Licensed Washington vehicle dealers have specific disclosure obligations for certain branded or total-loss vehicles.

Regardless of whether you are buying from a dealer or private seller, inspect the actual title and vehicle-history information.

If you later sell a rebuilt vehicle, accurately represent its title and total-loss history and comply with the ownership-transfer and disclosure requirements that apply to the transaction.
