Michigan Salvage Vehicle Guidance

Understand Michigan salvage, scrap, and rebuilt salvage titles, mechanic certification, salvage vehicle inspections, and whether a damaged vehicle can legally return to the road.

What you should know

Michigan distinguishes sharply between a vehicle with a salvage title, which can potentially be rebuilt for road use, and a vehicle with a scrap title, which cannot.

For a qualifying late-model distressed vehicle, Michigan generally requires a salvage title when estimated repair costs are 75% or more but less than 91% of the vehicle's pre-damaged actual cash value. At 91% or more, a scrap title is generally required.

Salvage can potentially return to the road. Scrap cannot.

A salvage-titled vehicle cannot be plated or used on public roads until it has been repaired, inspected by a certified mechanic, inspected and recertified by a specially trained law-enforcement salvage vehicle inspector, and retitled. If it successfully completes that process, Michigan issues a title branded REBUILT SALVAGE.

Understand Michigan terminology

Distressed vehicle

Michigan uses the term distressed vehicle for certain late-model vehicles with one or more major component parts that have been wrecked, destroyed, damaged, stolen, or are missing and whose estimated repair cost reaches the statutory threshold. For purposes of determining whether a late-model distressed vehicle requires a salvage or scrap title, estimated repair costs include parts and labor.

Late-model vehicle

Michigan's definition depends on vehicle weight.

  • a vehicle weighing less than 8,000 pounds that was manufactured within the last six model years, or
  • a vehicle weighing 8,000 pounds or more that was manufactured within the last 16 model years.

Because this definition changes over time and depends on the vehicle's weight, verify whether the particular vehicle qualifies as late model before relying on the percentage thresholds.

Salvage vehicle

For a late-model distressed vehicle, a salvage title is generally required when estimated repair costs are 75% or more but less than 91% of the vehicle's pre-damaged actual cash value. A vehicle with a salvage title can potentially be rebuilt for road use.

Michigan can also issue a salvage title in other circumstances, including when an owner voluntarily determines that a salvage title is appropriate.

Scrap vehicle

For a late-model distressed vehicle, a scrap title is generally required when estimated repair costs are 91% or more of the vehicle's pre-damaged actual cash value.

A scrap vehicle cannot be rebuilt for normal road use. Michigan states that after a scrap title is issued, the vehicle can be used only for parts or scrap metal. It cannot be registered and returned to the road.

Rebuilt salvage vehicle

A salvage vehicle that has been repaired and successfully completed Michigan's recertification process can receive a title branded REBUILT SALVAGE. That brand informs future owners that the vehicle previously had salvage status. Michigan states that title brands remain on future titles.

Can this vehicle return to the road?

Possibly—if it has a salvage title. A Michigan salvage-titled vehicle can potentially be repaired, inspected, recertified, retitled, registered, and returned to road use. A scrap-titled vehicle cannot.

That makes the first question particularly simple:

Does this vehicle have a salvage title or a scrap title?

If it has a salvage title, the next questions are:

Can the repairs meet Michigan's requirements?

Can you document the vehicle and the parts used to rebuild it?

Answer those questions before investing substantially in the project.

How do I get a Michigan salvage vehicle back on the road?

  1. Confirm that the vehicle has a salvage title rather than a scrap title.
  2. Repair or rebuild the vehicle.
  3. Keep ownership documentation for major component parts used during the rebuild.
  4. Complete the applicable portions of Form TR-13A, Application for Salvage Vehicle Inspection.
  5. Have the repairs inspected and certified by an appropriately certified mechanic as required by the TR-13A process.
  6. Arrange an inspection with a certified Michigan salvage vehicle inspector.
  7. Present the vehicle, TR-13A, and ownership documentation for applicable parts.
  8. Have the vehicle inspected for parts ownership and compliance with applicable Michigan equipment and safety requirements.
  9. If the vehicle passes, obtain the completed TR-13B Salvage Vehicle Recertification from the inspector.
  10. Take the salvage title, TR-13A, TR-13B, required identification, proof of Michigan no-fault insurance, and other required documents to a Secretary of State office.
  11. Complete the applicable title and registration process.
  12. If approved, receive a Michigan title branded REBUILT SALVAGE.

Use the current Michigan Secretary of State instructions and forms when completing the process because forms, fees, inspection procedures, and other requirements can change.

What is Form TR-13A?

Michigan's Application for Salvage Vehicle Inspection (TR-13A) is an important part of the recertification process.

The form documents the vehicle, the rebuilding work, and the required mechanic certification.

Michigan's current form requires repairs to be inspected and certified as having been performed in a workmanlike manner by a properly certified mechanic in the appropriate specialty.

At minimum, Michigan's current TR-13A instructions call for a mechanic certifying salvage repairs to hold the applicable certification categories for:

  • unitized body and structural repair
  • collision-related mechanical repair

Additional appropriately certified mechanics can be needed when repairs involve other specialties. The mechanic certification and the later salvage vehicle inspection perform different functions.

What does the salvage vehicle inspector check?

After the required repair certification is complete, the vehicle is inspected by a specially trained law-enforcement officer certified as a Michigan salvage vehicle inspector.

During the inspection, the owner must provide applicable ownership documentation for parts used to rebuild the vehicle.

The inspector verifies:

  • ownership of repair parts
  • identifying information associated with the vehicle and its components
  • applicable Michigan Vehicle Code equipment requirements
  • applicable safety requirements

If the vehicle passes, the inspector completes the Salvage Vehicle Recertification (TR-13B). That completed recertification becomes part of the rebuilt-title application.

Does passing the salvage inspection guarantee that the vehicle is a good purchase?

No.

Michigan's recertification process includes inspection for compliance with applicable equipment and safety requirements, and the TR-13A process requires mechanic certification of the repairs.

Those requirements are meaningful, but a rebuilt title should not be treated as a general warranty of the vehicle's condition, durability, or future reliability.

A prospective buyer should still independently evaluate issues such as:

  • structural repair quality
  • mechanical condition
  • steering and suspension
  • wheel alignment
  • electrical systems
  • airbags and restraint systems
  • corrosion or water damage
  • quality of replacement components
  • insurance availability
  • future resale implications

The state process answers whether the vehicle qualifies for recertification and a rebuilt title. A buyer still needs to determine whether the particular vehicle is a sensible purchase.

Why are parts documents important?

Michigan's salvage inspection specifically verifies ownership of parts used to repair the vehicle.

At the inspection, the owner must provide assigned titles or bills of sale for applicable parts used in rebuilding.

This documentation helps establish that major components were legally acquired and allows the inspector to compare the rebuilt vehicle with its repair and ownership records.

Michigan identifies numerous vehicle items as major component parts, including components such as the engine, transmission, body panels, doors, bumpers, frame-related components, and other major structural or body parts identified by state law.

If you are buying a partially rebuilt vehicle, ask for the parts documentation before completing the purchase. Missing documentation can become your problem when you try to complete the recertification process.

Can I drive a salvage vehicle to its inspection?

A salvage-titled vehicle cannot be used for ordinary public-road operation before it has been recertified and retitled.

Michigan law and current Secretary of State guidance provide a limited exception allowing a vehicle to be moved on the highway by the most direct route for purposes that include obtaining a salvage recertification inspection.

That exception should not be treated as ordinary permission to drive a salvage vehicle.

Before driving the vehicle to an inspection, verify the current requirements concerning the inspection appointment, route, insurance, vehicle condition, documentation, and any other requirements that apply.

What if the vehicle came from another state?

Michigan recognizes comparable salvage and scrap title classifications from other states and jurisdictions. The exact ownership document therefore matters.

Before buying an out-of-state project:

  • inspect the actual title or ownership document
  • identify every brand and restriction
  • determine whether Michigan considers the document comparable to salvage or scrap
  • determine whether the vehicle qualifies as a late-model vehicle
  • determine whether dealer or purchaser restrictions apply
  • verify whether Michigan will allow the vehicle to enter the rebuilt-salvage process

Do not rely solely on the words used in an auction listing.

Are there restrictions on buying late-model salvage vehicles?

Yes. Michigan's dealer guidance identifies restrictions involving late-model salvage vehicles.

The current Michigan dealer manual states that a Class C, Class H, or Class R dealer license is required to obtain a late-model salvage vehicle, including qualifying vehicles coming from another state.

This is important for an individual looking at an auction vehicle. Before bidding on or arranging the purchase of a late-model salvage vehicle, determine whether you are legally eligible to acquire that particular vehicle through the proposed transaction.

Do not assume that because an auction displays a vehicle to the public, every prospective buyer is legally eligible to acquire it.

What about stolen-and-recovered vehicles?

Michigan has special provisions for certain late-model stolen-and-recovered vehicles.

A late-model stolen vehicle owned by an insurance company licensed to do business in Michigan can require a salvage title when one or more major component parts were wrecked, destroyed, damaged beyond salvage, missing, or stolen. This requirement can apply regardless of the normal repair-cost percentage.

If you are considering a theft-recovery vehicle, investigate:

  • the actual title
  • whether major components were missing or damaged
  • what parts were replaced
  • whether the vehicle has already received a salvage title
  • whether parts documentation is complete
  • whether the vehicle must complete the salvage recertification process

The words “theft recovery” alone do not establish the vehicle's title status.

What about flood-damaged vehicles?

Michigan title records can identify flood-damaged vehicles, and flood-related title branding remains part of the vehicle's title history.

A vehicle that is legally eligible for rebuilding still needs a separate assessment of its physical condition.

For a flood-damaged vehicle, investigate issues such as:

  • corrosion in wiring and connectors
  • damage to electronic modules
  • water contamination
  • mold or interior damage
  • airbags and restraint-system components
  • engine and drivetrain exposure
  • long-term electrical reliability

A successful title or inspection process does not establish that hidden flood damage has been eliminated.

What should I check before buying a salvage vehicle in Michigan?

  • What is the exact ownership document?
  • Is the vehicle salvage or scrap?
  • Is it considered a late-model vehicle under Michigan law?
  • What percentage of its pre-damage value did the estimated repairs represent?
  • Why was it declared salvage?
  • Was it damaged in a collision, flood, fire, or theft?
  • What major component parts were damaged or missing?
  • Has rebuilding already begun?
  • Who performed the repairs?
  • Can the required mechanic certification be completed?
  • What major components have been replaced?
  • Are assigned titles, bills of sale, or other ownership documents available for those components?
  • Are the VIN and other identifying numbers intact?
  • Has the vehicle already undergone salvage inspection?
  • Can you legally acquire the vehicle through the proposed transaction?
  • Can you obtain the insurance coverage you want?
  • What repair, inspection, title, registration, tax, and transportation costs remain?
  • Has the quality and condition of completed repairs been independently evaluated?

The central Michigan question is:

Is this vehicle legally salvage rather than scrap, and can it successfully complete Michigan's repair-certification and salvage-recertification process?

What should I know if I later sell or transfer the vehicle?

A successfully recertified Michigan salvage vehicle receives a title branded REBUILT SALVAGE.

Michigan states that title brands remain on future titles, so rebuilding the vehicle does not erase its salvage history.

Michigan also has disclosure requirements associated with salvage and rebuilt vehicles, including requirements that can depend on the vehicle's age, weight, seller, and transaction.

If you later sell or transfer the vehicle:

  • accurately represent its REBUILT SALVAGE history
  • do not imply that the vehicle has an unbranded title
  • provide disclosures required for the particular transaction
  • complete the normal Michigan title-transfer requirements

If you are buying from a dealer, Michigan Secretary of State guidance specifically advises consumers that they are entitled to a salvage disclosure form for qualifying late-model vehicles.

What should I do next?

If you are thinking about buying a Michigan salvage vehicle:Inspect the actual ownership document first. Make sure it is salvage rather than scrap and determine whether Michigan considers it a late-model vehicle.
If you are considering a late-model auction vehicle:Determine whether Michigan's purchaser or dealer-license restrictions affect your ability to acquire the vehicle before bidding.
If the vehicle has already been partially rebuilt:Obtain the repair history and ownership documentation for replacement components before buying it.
If you are rebuilding the vehicle:Review TR-13A early rather than waiting until the repairs are complete. Make sure the work can receive the required mechanic certification and preserve the documentation needed for replacement parts.
If the vehicle is ready for inspection:Locate a certified Michigan salvage vehicle inspector and review the current Secretary of State inspection requirements.
If the vehicle has a scrap title:Do not invest in rebuilding it for highway use. Michigan does not allow a scrap-titled vehicle to be retitled and returned to the road.
