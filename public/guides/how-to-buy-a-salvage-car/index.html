How to Buy a Salvage Car

Buying a salvage vehicle can sometimes provide an opportunity to purchase a vehicle for less than a comparable vehicle with a clean title. But the purchase price is only one part of the decision.

Before buying, you need to understand why the vehicle received a salvage designation, what it may cost to repair, what your state requires before the vehicle can return to the road, and how the title history could affect insurance and resale later.

What you should know

A salvage vehicle can make sense when you understand the damage, the title status, the likely repair costs, and the requirements for registering the vehicle before you buy it.

The biggest risk is making a decision with incomplete information.

A low auction or purchase price does not necessarily mean a low total cost. Transportation, auction fees, repairs, inspections, parts, taxes, title fees, and unexpected damage can change the economics of a vehicle significantly.

Before committing to a purchase, investigate both the vehicle and the process required to make it usable in your state.

Understand what the title tells you

Start with the vehicle's title status.

States use title brands to describe important events or conditions in a vehicle's history. Terms can include salvage, rebuilt, flood, junk, and other designations. The terminology and requirements associated with those brands vary by state.

A salvage designation generally means that the vehicle experienced an event significant enough to affect its title status. It does not, by itself, tell you exactly what happened to the vehicle or how difficult it will be to repair.

That is why the title should be treated as the beginning of your research rather than the complete story.

Before buying, determine:

  • What title or ownership document comes with the vehicle?
  • Why was the vehicle declared a total loss or given its current title status?
  • Does the vehicle have previous title brands?
  • Can this particular vehicle legally be rebuilt and registered in your state?
  • What documentation will your state require?

Do not assume that a vehicle described as “salvage” in one state will be treated exactly the same way in another.

Research the vehicle's history

Use the vehicle identification number (VIN) to learn as much as possible about the vehicle before purchasing it.

A vehicle history report may provide information about title history, reported salvage or total-loss records, odometer readings, and other events associated with the vehicle.

The National Motor Vehicle Title Information System (NMVTIS) is an important source for title and brand information. Other commercial vehicle-history services may provide additional information.

No vehicle history report should be treated as a substitute for inspecting the actual vehicle.

Reports can be incomplete, damage may not have been recorded, and they cannot tell you the current quality of repairs or the condition of every component.

Understand the damage

Try to determine what caused the vehicle to become a salvage vehicle and how extensive the damage is.

Visible body damage may only be part of the problem. Depending on the vehicle and the event that damaged it, there may also be problems involving:

  • the frame or structural components
  • suspension and steering
  • airbags and other safety systems
  • electrical systems
  • sensors and cameras
  • cooling systems
  • drivetrain components
  • water intrusion or corrosion

Photos can help you evaluate a vehicle, particularly when buying through an online auction, but photos alone cannot establish the full condition of a vehicle.

Whenever possible, inspect the vehicle in person or have someone qualified evaluate it before buying.

Look beyond the purchase price

One of the easiest mistakes to make is comparing the price of a salvage vehicle directly with the retail value of a similar clean-title vehicle.

The more useful comparison is the total cost of getting the vehicle to the condition and legal status you want.

Depending on the purchase, that may include:

  • the vehicle purchase price
  • auction or buyer fees
  • transportation or towing
  • parts
  • labor
  • diagnostic work
  • structural or alignment work
  • inspections
  • title and registration fees
  • taxes
  • unexpected repairs

Leave room in your estimate for things you cannot see before the vehicle is disassembled or inspected more thoroughly.

A vehicle that appears inexpensive can become expensive quickly when several uncertain costs are added together.

Understand your state's requirements before you buy

This step should happen before purchasing the vehicle, not after.

Salvage and rebuilt-vehicle requirements are established primarily at the state level. States can differ in terminology, inspections, documentation, photographs, receipts, title procedures, and whether particular types of damaged vehicles can be returned to the road.

The state where you plan to title and register the vehicle may matter more to your decision than the state where you buy it.

Your state matters

Before buying a salvage vehicle, find out what your state requires to rebuild, inspect, title, and register it.

If the vehicle is coming from another state, also understand how its existing title or ownership document will be treated by the state where you plan to register it.

Think about insurance, financing, and future resale

Repairing a vehicle is not the end of the decision.

Consider how the vehicle's title history may affect what happens after it is rebuilt.

Before buying, investigate whether you will be able to obtain the type of insurance coverage you want. If you plan to finance the vehicle, find out whether a lender is willing to finance a vehicle with its particular title status.

You should also assume that title history will matter when you eventually sell or trade the vehicle.

The relevant question is therefore not simply:

Can I repair this vehicle?

It is:

Does buying, repairing, owning, insuring, and eventually selling this particular vehicle make sense for me?

Inspect before you commit

Whenever circumstances allow, arrange for an independent inspection before buying.

For an auction vehicle, a traditional pre-purchase inspection may not always be possible. In that situation, recognize the additional uncertainty and factor it into your decision and budget.

An inspection is particularly important because vehicle-history information and auction photos cannot reveal every mechanical, structural, electrical, or safety issue.

If you cannot adequately evaluate a vehicle yourself, getting help from someone who can may be one of the most valuable costs associated with the purchase.

Decide before you bid

Auctions can create pressure to make decisions quickly.

Try to make the important decisions before bidding begins.

Determine:

  • the most you are willing to pay for the vehicle
  • your estimated repair and transportation costs
  • the fees associated with the purchase
  • the state requirements you will need to satisfy
  • how much uncertainty you are willing to accept

Then establish your maximum purchase price based on those factors.

The winning bid is not necessarily a good deal simply because it is lower than the price of a comparable clean-title vehicle.

Sometimes the best decision is not to buy the vehicle.

Official sources

For title and vehicle-history research, useful official starting points include:

National Motor Vehicle Title Information System (NMVTIS)

NMVTIS is a federal vehicle-history system overseen by the U.S. Department of Justice and operated by the American Association of Motor Vehicle Administrators (AAMVA). It can provide title-brand history and certain junk, salvage, insurance total-loss, and odometer information.

Consumer reports are available through approved third-party NMVTIS providers and may require a fee. SalvageCars.com is not affiliated with NMVTIS, AAMVA, the U.S. Department of Justice, or its approved data providers.

Federal Trade Commission — Used Car Guidance

Consumer guidance on researching used vehicles, obtaining vehicle-history information, checking recalls, and arranging independent inspections.

For salvage-title, rebuilt-title, inspection, and registration requirements, use the official motor vehicle agency or other appropriate government source for the state where you intend to title and register the vehicle.

What should I do next?

Before committing to a purchase, make sure you understand the vehicle's history and condition, the complete cost of buying and repairing it, and whether it can be titled and registered in your state.

If you're evaluating a particular vehicle, start with your state's requirements. If you need more background on salvage and rebuilt classifications, read What Is a Salvage Car?
